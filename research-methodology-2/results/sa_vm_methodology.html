1

The task setup

Seven fixed, calibrated cameras film a 12 × 36 m public square. Each frame has about 20 people, and a point on the ground is seen by 3.74 cameras on average. We want two things: a dot on the ground for every person (detection), and the same ID for each person from frame to frame (tracking).

The ground truth is a top-down grid of 480 × 1440 cells, each 2.5 cm wide. Video runs at 2 frames per second. We train on frames 0–359 and test on frames 360–399, which hold 952 labelled people in total.

2

Why a map seen from above idea

A person hidden behind someone in one camera is usually visible in another. If we move every camera's information onto one shared top-down map, the bird's-eye view (BEV), the cameras can vote together on where people stand. This is the core idea behind TrackTacular and the methods it builds on.

3

Turn each image into features baseline

Each 720 × 1280 image goes through ResNet-18, a standard image network pre-trained on ImageNet. It outputs a feature map: 128 numbers per location on a grid 8 times smaller than the image (90 × 160). Each location's numbers describe what is there, such as "a leg" or "a head" or "pavement". All seven cameras share the same network.

4

Lift the features onto the map baseline

We cover the square with a 3D grid of small boxes, called voxels: 360 × 120 × 4 of them, each 10 cm × 10 cm × 50 cm, up to 2 m high. For every voxel and every camera, the calibration tells us which pixel that voxel lands on. We copy the feature found there into the voxel, blending the four nearest feature locations. This is called bilinear sampling, and it comes from Simple-BEV. Voxels a camera cannot see stay empty for that camera.

The key point for later: where a camera's evidence lands on the map depends only on the geometry, meaning the calibration and the map transform. Change the map transform, and that camera's evidence moves.

5

Fuse the seven cameras baseline

For each voxel, the seven cameras' features are stacked side by side, always in the same camera order, then mixed by a small 3D convolution. Because the order is fixed, the network can learn how much to trust each camera. The four height levels are then folded into one, giving a 120 × 360 top-down feature map.

6

Predict people, and where they just were baseline

Small output layers called heads read the map and predict three things:

  • a center heatmap, high where a person stands;
  • a sub-cell offset, the exact position inside the 10 cm cell;
  • a tracking offset, where this person was one frame earlier.

Training compares these predictions to the labels using standard losses: a focal loss for the heatmap and L1-type losses for the offsets.

7

From heatmap to tracks baseline

Every heatmap peak above 0.5 is a detection. The tracker then takes each detection's predicted previous position and matches it to the nearest existing track, so the person keeps their ID. Unmatched detections start new tracks.

8

Flip the whole scene during training borrowed: MVAug

We only have 360 training frames, so the network sees the same crowd layouts again and again. Scene augmentation creates new layouts. With probability 0.5, we mirror the map left–right, top–bottom, or both. For example, a person at x = 300 on the 1440-wide grid moves to x = 1140.

We do not flip the images. We change the map transform instead, so every camera's evidence lands in the mirrored position, and we move the labels the same way. Images, evidence and labels stay consistent with each other. MVAug introduced this and showed it reduces overfitting.

9

View mixing: three cameras get the other sample's flip ours, found by accident

Training uses batches of two frames. In the code we trained with, the map transform was copied to the cameras in the wrong order. As a result, cameras 2, 4 and 6 of each frame were placed using the other frame's flip. (The code counts cameras from 0, so these are cameras 1, 3 and 5 there.) The labels stayed correct.

When both frames have the same flip, nothing changes. That happens 1/3 of the time. The other 2/3 of the time, those three cameras put their evidence at mirrored positions, while the other four cameras agree with the labels. To find people, the network has to trust the cameras that agree and ignore the ones that conflict. That is similar to training with badly calibrated cameras. At test time every camera is placed correctly.

We later fixed the order (A2f). Same settings and same seed, and tracking got worse: MOTA fell from 92.65 to 90.76. So the "bug" was helping. We now keep it as a deliberate training option (--model.view_mix).

10

Random view mixing ours, new in methodology 3

The accidental version always misplaces the same three cameras, which looks like a quirk. The deliberate version picks cameras at random: during training, each camera takes the other frame's flip with probability 3/7. On average that is the same three cameras as before, but a different three every time.

If this works as well or better, view mixing is a general principle, not an accident of which cameras were hit. The code option is --model.view_mix_random 0.4286, and the variant is called RVM.

Result (session 1, one seed): it did not work. RVM got 90.65 MODA, 90.86 MOTA and 94.64 IDF1, against 92.02, 92.65 and 95.42 for fixed mixing at the same seed. It missed 62 people, against 53. Under small calibration errors it collapsed to 43 MODA. Random mixing is not a replacement for fixed mixing, and we do not yet know why the two behave so differently.

11

Mirror test-time flips ours, applied at test time

Our model was trained on all four mirror versions of the map. At test time we run it four times, once per mirror, flip each heatmap back, and average them. Averaging four slightly different opinions removes some noise in the heatmap. A plain TrackTacular never saw mirrored maps, so for it we expect no gain. That makes it a check on our explanation.

The flip is exact on this grid: cell i maps onto cell 359 − i, with no shift. It needs no retraining, and 40 test frames take seconds.

Result: it hurt every model. SA-VM lost 4.8 to 6.4 MODA, and scene augmentation without mixing lost about 2. Averaging four heatmaps lowers the peak for people that some mirrors miss, so more peaks fall below the 0.5 threshold and become misses. The plain TrackTacular lost 37 to 46 MODA, as expected for a model that never saw mirrored maps. We dropped mirror TTA.

12

Stress tests ours, evaluation only

If view mixing teaches the network to ignore cameras that disagree, it should cope better when the real calibration is slightly wrong, or when a camera fails. We test both on the same 40 test frames:

  • Calibration noise. Each camera's position and angle are nudged at random, by 0.5° and 5 cm (low) or 2° and 20 cm (high). These sizes come from ATLASFusion, which found that plain TrackTacular drops to 50.5 MODA at the low level and 0 at the high level. Every model gets the same three random nudges.
  • Camera failure. Switch off each single camera (7 tests), then 7 pairs of cameras.

The checkpoints of our 3 retrained TrackTacular runs and 3 SA-VM runs from attempt 1 are still on Drive. So this comparison gets 3 seeds without any new training.

Control: camera dropout (CD) switches cameras off during training instead of misplacing them, at a matched rate. If CD does as well as view mixing, our idea is just dropout under another name. If it does worse, misplacing evidence teaches something that removing it does not.

Result (3 seeds, SA-VM vs our retrained TrackTacular): with small calibration errors SA-VM is ahead on every seed, by 5.8 MOTA and 9.0 IDF1 on average. That is suggestive, but with 3 seeds it is not statistically significant (p ≈ 0.06–0.08). With large errors every model fails, and SA-VM fails worse. When cameras are switched off SA-VM loses more than the baseline (2 cameras off: −1.75 MODA, p = 0.012). It already misses a few more people on clean data, and missing cameras make that worse. Camera dropout (CD) has not been run yet.

13

How we score it

A detection counts as correct if it is within 0.5 m of a labelled person.

  • MODA = 1 − (missed people + false detections) / 952. One error costs about 0.105 points.
  • MOTA is the same but on the tracker's output, and it also counts ID switches.
  • IDF1 measures how consistently each person keeps one ID.

We follow the paper's protocol exactly: threshold 0.5, TrackTacular's tracker, last training epoch, nothing tuned on the test frames.

14

What we found

We trained each model three times with different random seeds (1, 2, 80085), because results move by 0.3–1.5 points from seed to seed. Against our own retrained TrackTacular, SA-VM wins on every seed and every metric. Against the paper's single run it is level: none of the differences is statistically meaningful. A second seed of scene augmentation without mixing confirms that the mixing is what matters: removing it costs 3.05 MOTA at seed 2 and 1.89 at seed 80085. The pattern behind the gain is fewer false detections (15 fewer per test set) at the cost of a few more misses (8 more). The tables are in the paper version.

3  Method

We build on TrackTacular [1] with bilinear-sampling lifting [5]. We leave the architecture unchanged and modify only training and inference.

  • §3.1 states the problem and §3.2 summarises the baseline.
  • §3.3 describes scene augmentation [3].
  • §3.4–3.5 introduce cross-sample view mixing in a fixed and a random form.
  • §3.6 describes mirror test-time augmentation.
  • §3.7 defines the robustness protocol.

3.1  Problem formulation

A scene is observed by V = 7 static cameras with intrinsics Kv and extrinsics Tv = [Rv | tv]. A fixed similarity G maps world coordinates to the reference (grid) frame of the ground plane, discretised into X × Y = 360 × 120 cells of 10 cm, with Z = 4 height bins up to 2 m. Given synchronised images I1:V at time t, the model predicts pedestrian ground positions and their displacement to t − 1. A tracker links these into identities.

3.2  Baseline

Lifting. A shared ResNet-18 encoder Φ produces features Φ(Iv) ∈ ℝ128 × H/8 × W/8. For a voxel centre x in the reference frame, camera v contributes

fv(x) = Φ(Iv)⟨ π( K̃v Tv G−1 x ) ⟩(1)

Here π is the perspective division, K̃v is the intrinsic matrix scaled to the feature resolution, and ⟨·⟩ is bilinear interpolation. Voxels outside the image get zero and a validity mask mv(x) = 0.

Fusion and decoding. Camera features are concatenated in a fixed camera order and fused by a 3D convolution ψ. Height is folded into channels, and a 2D compressor ρ gives the BEV feature map

B = ρ( foldZ( ψ([ f1 ; … ; fV ]) ) ) ∈ ℝC × Y × X(2)

A decoder predicts a center heatmap Ĥ, a sub-cell offset Ô and a tracking offset Δ̂ to the person's position at t − 1. An auxiliary head predicts image-plane foot heatmaps. The training loss is the uncertainty-weighted sum [1] of a focal loss on Ĥ, L1 losses on Ô, a smooth-L1 loss on Δ̂ and a focal loss on the image heatmaps.

Inference. Detections are local maxima of Ĥ above τ = 0.5. The tracker associates each detection with existing tracks by the distance between the track position and the detection shifted by Δ̂.

3.3  Scene augmentation

Following MVAug [3], we augment the reference frame rather than the images. For training sample b we draw

Gb = Ab Jb G,   Ab ∈ { I, C Sx C−1, C Sy C−1, C Sxy C−1 }(3)

Here S· are the axis mirrors diag(−1, 1), diag(1, −1) and diag(−1, −1). C translates to the grid centre (720, 240), so the grid maps onto itself. Jb is a translation jitter drawn uniformly from ±0.25 grid units, as in the baseline. Ab = I with probability 0.5, and otherwise one of the three mirrors uniformly. The same transform is applied to the labels of frames t and t − 1:

yb = Ab Jb y,   ybt−1 = Ab Jb yt−1(4)

Images and image-plane labels are unchanged. Because Eq. (1) uses Gb in place of G for every camera, the lifted evidence of all views moves with the labels. This scene-consistent geometry is what MVAug's scene augmentation preserves [3, §3].

3.4  Cross-sample view mixing

Let a training batch hold B samples with reference frames G0:B−1. View mixing lifts camera v of sample b with the frame of sample μ(b, v):

fbv(x) = Φ(Ibv)⟨ π( K̃v Tv (Gμ(b,v))−1 x ) ⟩,   μ(b, v) = (bV + v) mod B(5)

The labels remain yb. With B = 2 and odd V, μ(b, v) = (b + v) mod 2. The cameras with odd index (1, 3, 5) take the partner's frame, and the cameras with even index (0, 2, 4, 6) keep their own. A mixed camera is therefore misregistered by

E = Gμ (Gb)−1 = Aμ Jμ (Ab Jb)−1(6)

E is the identity up to sub-centimetre jitter when both samples drew the same mirror. That happens with probability 0.5² + 0.5²/3 = 1/3. Otherwise E is a mirror or a 180° rotation about the grid centre. In two thirds of the batches, three of the seven views therefore carry evidence that conflicts with the labels, while four agree. Because fusion (Eq. 2) is camera-ordered, the network can learn to rely on cross-view agreement and to discount views that disagree. At inference μ(b, v) = b, so the geometry is exact. Eq. (5) is exactly the camera-to-frame assignment produced by a repeat-for-repeat_interleave ordering error in our attempt-1 code. We keep it as an explicit option.

Algorithm 1  One training step of SA-VM (B = 2)
  1. for b in {0, 1}: draw Jb; with prob. 0.5 draw a mirror Ab, else Ab ← I; Gb ← Ab Jb G
  2. yb, ybt−1 ← Ab Jb (y, yt−1) ▹ labels follow the scene, Eq. (4)
  3. for each camera v: μ ← (b + v) mod 2 (fixed), or μ ← 1 − b with prob. q, else b (random); lift Φ(Ibv) with (Gμ)−1 ▹ Eq. (5), (7)
  4. fuse cameras and decode Ĥ, Ô, Δ̂ ▹ Eq. (2)
  5. L ← focal(Ĥ, yb) + L1(Ô) + smoothL1(Δ̂, ybt−1) + image loss; accumulate gradients over 8 batches

3.5  Random view mixing

The fixed assignment of Eq. (5) always perturbs the same cameras. Since fusion is camera-ordered, the network could in principle learn camera-specific distrust rather than agreement-based reasoning. Random view mixing removes this confound:

μ(b, v) = { b′ with prob. q;  b otherwise },   b′ = (b + 1) mod B,   q = 3/7(7)

The draws are independent for every sample and camera. With V = 7, the choice q = 3/7 matches the expected number of mixed cameras in Eq. (5). Misregistration still occurs only when the two samples drew different mirrors (probability 2/3). As a control, we replace mixing with camera dropout [5]: 67 % of samples lose 1–3 random cameras. That rate is matched to the fraction of batches in which Eq. (5) misplaces cameras.

3.6  Mirror test-time augmentation

Models trained with Eq. (3) have seen all four mirrors of the reference frame. At inference we evaluate the network under each mirror A ∈ 𝒜 = {I, Sx, Sy, Sxy} (about the grid centre), map the heatmaps back and average them:

ĤTTA = ¼ ΣA ∈ 𝒜 flipA( Ĥ( I1:V; A G ) )(8)

Voxel i is centred at xmin + (i + ½)·s, so the mirror maps cell i to cell X − 1 − i exactly, and flipA is an index reversal. Offsets and tracking offsets are taken from the identity pass. Eq. (8) is reported separately from the protocol numbers because it changes inference. We expect it to help scene-augmented models but not the baseline, which never saw mirrored frames.

3.7  Robustness protocol

Calibration noise. Each camera's extrinsics are perturbed in its own frame, T′v = Nv Tv. Nv combines an axis-angle rotation with i.i.d. Gaussian components of std σr and a translation with i.i.d. Gaussian components of std σt. We use (σr, σt) = (0.5°, 0.05 m) and (2.0°, 0.2 m), the low and high magnitudes of [4, Tab. 5], with three fixed draws per level shared by all models.

Camera failure. We switch off each single camera, and then 7 cyclic camera pairs.

All conditions use the protocol of §3.9, and models are compared on identical perturbations.

3.8  Implementation details

We use ResNet-18 pre-trained on ImageNet, input 720 × 1280 with random resize in [0.8, 1.2] and crop jitter [5], and a voxel grid of 360 × 120 × 4 over 36 × 12 × 2 m. We train for 30 epochs with Adam and a one-cycle schedule (peak learning rate 10−3), batch size 2 with gradient accumulation over 8 (effective 16), gradient clipping 0.5, fp32, no temporal feature cache, and a scene-augmentation probability of 0.5. A run takes about 24 minutes on one A100-80GB.

3.9  Evaluation protocol

We train on WILDTRACK [6] frames 0–359 and test on frames 360–399 (952 annotated detections). A detection is a true positive within r = 0.5 m [1]. We report MODA and MODP for detection, and MOTA and IDF1 for tracking, from the last epoch with τ = 0.5 and TrackTacular's tracker. No parameter is tuned on the test frames. Each configuration is trained with seeds {1, 2, 80085}. We compare against a baseline retrained under identical settings with a paired t-test over seeds, and against published single runs with a one-sample t-test.

Mirror TTA (Eq. 8) lowers MODA for every checkpoint, by 4.8–6.4 for SA-VM and 37–46 for the baseline; the loss is almost entirely missed detections, because averaging lowers peak scores below the fixed threshold. We therefore drop it. Random view mixing (§3.5) is worse than fixed mixing at the same seed (−1.37 MODA, −1.79 MOTA) and far less robust. Under low calibration noise SA-VM leads the baseline on all three seeds in MOTA and IDF1, while scene augmentation without mixing does not (−2.40 MODA), so the lead is due to mixing; it does not reach significance with three seeds. Under high noise all models fail, consistent with TrackTacular's 0.0 MODA in [4, Tab. 5]. Under camera failure SA-VM degrades more than the baseline (degradation difference −2.48 MODA for two cameras off, p = 0.002). With 8.3 more misses and 15.3 fewer false positives on clean data (p = 0.029 and 0.020), SA-VM appears to require agreement among more views; this explains both its clean tracking gain and its camera-failure loss, but we have not tested it directly. ATLASFusion's noise sampling is not specified, so our calibration numbers are comparable to theirs in magnitude only.

4.1  Limitations

The ablation in Table 2 has two seeds for A2f (2 and 80085) and one for the other rows. SA-VM was trained in the attempt-1 code; a retrain in the current code (A2x) and the camera-dropout control are pending. Random view mixing has one seed. Our novelty check covers only the 20 papers we downloaded. None of them lifts a subset of views with a deliberately wrong reference frame: MVAug's view augmentation keeps views aligned [3, §3], and Simple-BEV uses camera dropout [5]. The calibration-robustness lead (Table 4) is consistent but not significant with three seeds. Finally, the test split has 40 frames, so one error moves MODA by 0.105 points.
